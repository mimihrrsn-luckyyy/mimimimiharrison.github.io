thot ramblings

Existing is so strange, and perceiving seems like such a mystery. There is surely an infinite amount of information that our senses fail to perceive. Even things we can perceive, such as heat (with a sensitivity suited to this earthly range, LOL), fail to be organized spatially like visual input in.

I can feel on my skin the difference between the warm summer sun and the cold winter wind. I know that my bedroom is much warmer than the hallway, and I know that the garage is even warmer than my bedroom.

Yet, outside of my home, I definitely don't perceive the space I travel through thermally. Even just now, I almost typed: "I cannot visualize space in the same thermal way". Language itself is so intimately connected to our visual experience of the world.

It reminds me of Clarice Lispector's Agua Viva:

"What I'm writing to you is not for reading—it's for being."

"Could it be that what I'm writing to you is beyond thought?"

"Beyond thought there are no words: it is itself."

"I know that my gaze must be that of a primitive person surrendered completely to the world, primitive like the gods who only allow the broad strokes of good and evil and don't want to know about good tangled up like hair in evil, evil that is good."

I think our senses must be severely limited by our earthly evolution and survival needs; I'm sure reality must far exceed the scope of human senses. Could we ever express in human language, which is itself developed from the sensory experience of the physical world, what the imagination cannot erect? Maybe a baby can imagine something beyond the sensory experience, but after decades alive, I can't.

I was thinking about all this because I was thinking about "ancient cycads" and their equally antiquated beetle pollinators. Cycads have existed for hundreds of millions of years, and of course, predate the existence of angiosperms. Why did humans think till the 1980's that prehistoric pollination would have to abide by the same rules as what we observe today in the angiosperm-dominant world? Cycads, unlike angiosperms, are believed to rely solely on thermogenesis as a pollination signal. Their pollinating-beetle companion poorly discriminates among color cues. Instead, the pollinating beetle detects infrared radiation with specialized sensilla at distances far beyond the range of metabolic cues such as scent volatility.

However, if a human encountered a cycad without prior knowledge, it would just appear as a ridiculously large pine cone embedded in a spikey, tropical-looking plant. For millions of years, we were oblivious to the fact that these plants are the ancient ancestors of modern flowering plants. If I were ignorant of this and designing a pollinator garden, I could even replace a cycad with a flowering plant- completely BLIND to the fact that cycads are not only endangered in the modern era, but the pinnacle and predecessor of all insect-pollinated plants.

Even on earth, where we have evolved for an imperceptible length of time, we are blind to much of the physical world we interact with, not only in the "oblivious" sense, but also in the sense that our sensory experience is extremely limited and dangerously misleading. Is the perception of time a sense too?

The fact that we believe that "to see = to truly experience the physical world", leads us to mistake what is visible as the entirety of reality.

It reminds me of a sentence I read in Siddhartha that stuck with me for a similar reason:

"The sun and moon had always shone, the rivers had always flowed and the bees had hummed, but in previous times all this had been nothing to Siddhartha but a fleeting and illusive viel before his eyes, regarded with distrust and condemed to be disregarded and ostracized from the thoughts, because it was not reality, because reality lay on the other side of the visible."

- > "reality lay on the other side of the visible."

A spec of dust compared to the magnitude of all that we cannot perceive and interpret. Imagine all that must be, that we will never be aware even exists. Originally, I think this line stuck with me because it made me think about physics and the span of the electromagnetic spectrum in a much broader sense. Now I feel like it means more to me, in a more metaphysical sense.

What does it even mean for something to be "invisible"? We KNOW that more exists; we can feel warmth, humidity, etc. Sensilla in our skin are so intertwined that all we can do is label these feelings as one sense, "touch," despite touch being comprised of so many specific senses of different stimuli. Another linguistic limitation on our ability to articulate an understanding of the physical, human experience. It almost feels to me like the ability to feel temperature, as a sense interpreted so differently from vision, is a tease that there is so much that cannot be sensed. I could scope out a room and enter inside, but if my body is numb, I have no idea what the room is really like. What if I entered my bedroom, unable to feel my body? Then suddenly, the ability to feel "touch" returned, and my room was 140 degrees. I would have been entirely blind to the state of my room, yet completely able to see the entire time. Vision is nothing, yet we think in vision, and so our minds convince us it is everything. Our bodies are covered in an array of sensilla just like the beetles, yet we lack the ability to spatially construct our experience as such.

As soon as we internalize that to see the world is to know the world, we become blind to all that lies outside. Maybe to be aware of imperceivable realities, though, could making one a slightly more aware observer? or at least a slightly more appreciative observer
